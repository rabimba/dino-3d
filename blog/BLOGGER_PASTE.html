Anyone who lives inside a browser knows that small jolt of betrayal when airport Wi-Fi drops mid-tab, softened a second later by Sebastien Gabriel, Alan Bettes, and Edward Jung’s pixelated Lonely T-Rex standing above ERR_INTERNET_DISCONNECTED. Since 2014, Chrome’s Offline Dino has been the web’s shared waiting room: two buttons, one flat horizon line, saguaro cacti, swooping pterodactyls, and a two-tone 100-point square-wave chime.

When I sat down to test Gemini Carbon, I skipped isolated algorithm puzzles and CRUD dashboards in favor of a real-time 3D browser game. Games expose spatial and systems mistakes immediately. Flip one sign in your coordinate handedness and the camera faces backward. Leave jump gravity too floaty and the controls feel dead. Misjudge bloom against sunlit desert sand and the whole viewport washes out to white.

The Gemini Carbon One-Shot Capability Test

Give Gemini Carbon a single creative brief—“faithfully reproduce Chrome Dino, then break it open into a progressive 3D browser platformer-runner culminating in falling meteors”—and see if it can design the 5-Act story arc, invent cohesive gameplay twists, and one-shot the complete playable 3D game in pure TypeScript, Three.js, and synthesized WebAudio with zero external models, textures, or audio files.

That prompt produced Signal Lost: Extinction Run (GitHub source): a 152 KB gzipped, 60 FPS WebGL2 platformer-runner backed by 19 deterministic Vitest specs, running straight from GitHub Pages across desktop keyboards, mobile touchscreens, and gamepads.

1. The Storyline: Five Escalating Acts in “Signal Lost: Extinction Run”

My main design rule for the prompt was simple: do not dump meteor storms and four-button platforming onto the player at score = 0. Chrome Dino works because onboarding takes half a second—you press SPACE, clear a cactus, and understand the rules. A 3D version needed to start with that exact simplicity and earn its complexity as your score climbs.

In src/core/StoryDirector.ts, a score-driven state machine controls the world’s palette, fog density, chunk verticality, and hazard pool. Whenever the connection drops, Lonely Rex starts frozen inside a flat 1-bit diagnostic screen. Pressing SPACE breaks that 2D plane open, swinging the camera 75° behind Rex as flat monochrome sand warms into a prehistoric canyon across five acts:

2. Inside the Zero-Asset 152 KB Engine: 120 Hz Physics, Beveled Voxels & WebAudio

Gemini Carbon separated the deterministic 120 Hz simulation core (src/core/, which has zero Three.js or DOM imports) from the WebGL2 rendering and WebAudio synthesis layers. Rex stays anchored near Z = 0 while canyon chunks, geysers, and meteors stream toward +Z, preventing floating-point precision drift during long high-score runs:

Three implementation choices carry most of the game-feel inside that loop:

  • Arcade Gravity & Graze Windows (DinoController.ts & CollisionSystem.ts): Earth gravity (-9.8 m/s²) makes an endless runner feel underwater, so Rex runs under -60 u/s² gravity with a 0.48× upward-velocity cut on early key release for short hops, a 3.3× mid-air Ground-Pound, 100 ms ledge coyote time, and an 80% inner hurtbox. Skimming within 0.85 units of an obstacle triggers NEAR MISS +25!, shaves 1.0s off Roar-Dash cooldown, and adds +18% to your Extinction Rage meter.
  • Selective HDR Bloom (Game.ts & DinoMesh.ts): Pinning the UnrealBloomPass threshold at 1.02 keeps sunlit sandstone and Rex’s charcoal voxels crisp without glare. Only materials pushed above 1.0 luminance—magma boulder cracks, golden Amber orbs, cyan Wi-Fi lasers, and incoming meteors—catch the glow.
  • Procedural WebAudio Synthesis (SoundManager.ts): Every sound effect is synthesized live in the browser: square-wave pitch sweeps for jumps, Chrome’s two-note 100-point milestone chime, filtered noise bursts when boulders shatter, a reverse-swept oscillator bank during Time-Rewind, and low-frequency thuds under Mega-Rex’s footsteps.

3. Turning Browser Lore into Mechanics: Three Gameplay Twists

Once the base runner felt responsive, I asked Gemini Carbon for twists that grew naturally out of the offline-browser premise and rewarded risky play.

Twist #1: The Reconnecting Wi-Fi Router Satellite Boss

Chrome’s Offline Dino lives inside a funny paradox: if your Wi-Fi ever comes back, the tab reloads and Rex’s world disappears. At 620 points (and every 1,050 points after), a giant voxel Wi-Fi Router Satellite with pulsing signal arcs drops into the sky ahead of the canyon under a WI-FI SIGNAL DETECTED — RECONNECTING... alert.

In WifiBossSystem.ts, the satellite locks cyan scan-lasers onto individual lanes while dropping ERR_404 monoliths and glowing Signal Relay pylons onto the track. Lining up with a relay and hitting SHIFT (Roar-Dash) shatters the pylon, short-circuits the satellite’s reconnection bar, and scores +150 CONNECTION REFUSED!. Smash three relays and the router spins back into orbit:

Figure 4: Fighting to stay offline. A 3D Wi-Fi Router Satellite sweeps cyan lasers down the track while Rex jams dropped Signal Relays with [SHIFT] Roar-Dash.

Twist #2: Prehistoric Amber 2.0s Time-Rewind & Your Crowned Ghost Rival

Losing a 1,900-point run to one late lane switch hurts, yet a passive extra-hit shield flattens tension. Placing Prehistoric Amber Crystals along elevated Tier-1 and Tier-2 bridges turns survival into an active route choice: collect three crystals in ChronoGhostSystem.ts, and a golden Chrono-Orb orbits Rex’s shoulders.

That same system records a 60 Hz ring buffer of your last 130 kinematic frames alongside your best saved run in localStorage. On your next attempt, your personal best sprints down the canyon beside you as a translucent cyan Ghost Dino wearing a golden voxel crown, firing NEW RECORD — GHOST OVERTAKEN! the instant you pass it. And if you clip a cactus or meteor while holding an Amber orb, the orb shatters and scrubs the entire 3D world backward for 2.0 seconds—sliding obstacles back down -Z and pulling falling meteors back up toward the clouds so you can steer into a clean lane: 

// Deterministic 2.0-second reverse timeline scrub in ChronoGhostSystem.ts
const targetIdx = Math.max(0, Math.floor((1 - t) * this.rewindStartSnapshotIndex));
const snap = this.rewindBuffer[targetIdx];
const worldShiftZ = -snap.runSpeed * dt * (CONFIG.REWIND_HISTORY_SEC / CONFIG.REWIND_DURATION_REAL_SEC);

for (const obs of obstacles) obs.z += worldShiftZ; // Push canyon hazards backward down -Z!
for (const m of meteors) {
  m.z += worldShiftZ;
  if (!m.impacted) m.elapsed = Math.max(0, m.elapsed - dt * 2.0); // Pull meteors back into the sky!
}

Twist #3: Act IV Meteor Skyfall, Obsidian Ramps & 2.35× Giant Mega-Rex Overdrive

At 1,600 points, the run reaches Act IV: Chicxulub Skyfall. A fractured comet blazes across a crimson sky while meteors rain toward the canyon ahead. To keep high-speed storms fair, MeteorSystem.ts projects a pulsing bullseye reticle onto the sand 1.3 seconds before impact, giving you time to strafe clear. One in four impacts also vitrifies into an angled Obsidian Launch Ramp, turning yesterday’s crater into a jump pad toward high-tier Amber.

Aggressive play feeds directly into the climax. Smashing boulders (+28%), grazing hazards (+18%), and collecting Amber (+14%) charge your Extinction Rage Meter. Press [R] at 100%—or let it auto-fire as a last-second save on a lethal hit—and Rex scales 2.35× into Giant Volcanic Mega-Rex while CameraRig.ts widens to a 62° Kaiju framing. For 7.5 seconds, cacti, boulders, and 404 monoliths flatten under Rex’s feet (+40 MEGA STOMP!), and jumping into a falling meteor headbutts it back into orbit for +120 METEOR HEADBUTT!:

4. What One-Shotting This Game Showed About Gemini Carbon

What stood out most during this test was how tightly Gemini Carbon connected theme to mechanics. Every story beat landed as playable math: Chrome’s flat #f7f7f7 error screen became a 75° camera orbit into 3D, the fear of reconnecting Wi-Fi became a relay-jamming boss fight, and Act IV’s meteors reshaped the track itself through obsidian ramps. Building every character and prop procedurally in VoxelBuilder.ts trades away smooth skeletal mesh deformation for blocky, beveled silhouettes. Even inside that constraint, counter-rotating hip joints, ground-projected drop shadows, and wide O_O shocked eyes on crash gave Rex plenty of personality while keeping the full production bundle at 152 KB gzipped.

The other big takeaway was how much mileage Gemini Carbon got out of pairing unit-tested physics with live visual inspection. Keeping DinoController, CollisionSystem, and StoryDirector free of Three.js let 19 Vitest specs lock down jump apexes, swept AABBs, and 1-tick Euler coyote-time edge cases in milliseconds. Unit tests still cannot tell you if a UI card blocks the camera, though. Two framing bugs only surfaced when Gemini Carbon inspected live headless Chrome WebGL renders: the centered Game Over overlay originally hid Rex’s shocked O_O face on crash, and scaling 2.35× into Mega-Rex clipped his dorsal crest against the top HUD until CameraRig added the 62° FOV pullback. Having the model run the game in a real browser, spot both framing collisions visually, and patch them in the same loop closed the gap between “compiles cleanly” and “feels finished.”
