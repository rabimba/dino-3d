Every person who spends their life inside a browser knows that tiny jolt of betrayal when airport Wi-Fi drops mid-tab—immediately softened by the sight of Sebastien Gabriel, Alan Bettes, and Edward Jung’s pixelated Lonely T-Rex standing above ERR_INTERNET_DISCONNECTED. Since 2014, Chrome’s Offline Dino has been humanity’s universal waiting room: two buttons, one flat horizon line, saguaro cacti, swooping pterodactyls, and that unmistakable two-tone 100-point square-wave chime.

When I sat down to stress-test the end-to-end engineering and creative capabilities of Gemini Carbon, I didn’t want to feed it another isolated algorithm benchmark or a boilerplate CRUD dashboard. Real-time 3D games expose every seam in a model’s spatial and systems reasoning at once: if your coordinate handedness drifts by a minus sign, the camera looks backward; if your jump gravity feels floaty, the controls feel dead; if bloom thresholds aren’t tuned against sunlit desert sand, the screen washes out to white; and if difficulty spikes too early, players bounce in ten seconds.

The Gemini Carbon One-Shot Capability Test

Could Gemini Carbon take a single high-level creative brief—“faithfully reproduce Chrome Dino, then break it open into a progressive 3D browser platformer-runner culminating in falling meteors”—chalk out a complete 5-Act narrative arc, invent cohesive meta-gameplay twists, and one-shot the entire playable 3D game in pure TypeScript, Three.js, and synthesized WebAudio with zero external 3D models, textures, or audio files?

The result is Signal Lost: Extinction Run (GitHub source): a 152 KB gzipped, 60 FPS browser runner backed by 19 deterministic Vitest physics and collision specs that runs directly from GitHub Pages on desktop keyboards, mobile touchscreens, and gamepads.

1. The Storyline: “Signal Lost: Extinction Run” Across Five Escalating Acts

The first design constraint I gave Gemini Carbon was that the game couldn’t dump meteor showers and complex multi-button platforming onto the player at score = 0. Part of Chrome Dino’s magic is its effortless onboarding: you press SPACE, you jump a cactus, and you immediately understand the contract. Our 3D reimagining needed a real narrative and mechanical arc that starts just as approachable and steadily raises the stakes until the sky itself starts falling.

In src/core/StoryDirector.ts, the world is governed by a continuous score-driven narrative state machine. Whenever your browser loses its connection, Lonely Rex wakes up trapped inside a frozen 1-bit monochrome diagnostic screen. Your first jump shatters that flat 2D plane, expanding the horizon into a living prehistoric valley whose colors, topography, atmospheric fog, and hazards evolve continuously across five story acts:

2. Inside the Zero-Asset 152 KB Engine: Pure 120 Hz Physics, Beveled Voxels & WebAudio DSP

One of the cleanest architectural choices Gemini Carbon made was strictly decoupling the deterministic 120 Hz simulation core (src/core/—zero Three.js or DOM imports, 100% unit-tested in Vitest) from the procedural WebGL2 presentation and WebAudio layers. Every obstacle, platform, geyser, and meteor is simulated around a stationary player origin (Z = 0) while world chunks stream toward +Z, avoiding floating-point jitter even on marathon multi-thousand-point runs:

Three engineering details in that pipeline make the game feel crisp rather than like a stiff tech demo:

  • Arcade Gravity & Forgiving Graze Math (DinoController.ts & CollisionSystem.ts): Real-world -9.8 m/s² gravity makes endless runners feel underwater. We run snappy -60 u/s² gravity paired with a 0.48× upward-velocity cutoff on early button release (so quick taps yield crisp low hops while holds clear tall saguaros), a 3.3× mid-air Ground-Pound fast-drop, 100 ms ledge coyote time, and an 80% inner hurtbox. Grazing within 0.85 units of a hazard awards NEAR MISS +25!, refunds 1.0s of Roar-Dash cooldown, and pumps +18% into your Extinction Rage meter.
  • Selective HDR Emissive Bloom (Game.ts & DinoMesh.ts): Setting UnrealBloomPass threshold to 1.02 keeps daylight desert sand and Rex’s beveled charcoal voxels razor-sharp with zero washout, while selectively blooming HDR emissive materials—glowing orange boulder seams, golden Amber Chrono-Orbs, cyan Wi-Fi scan lasers, and incoming molten bolides.
  • 100% Synthesized Retro-Modern Audio (SoundManager.ts): Every sound is generated mathematically in WebAudio: square-wave pitch ramps for single and double jumps, Chrome’s faithful two-note 100-point chime, band-passed noise bursts for boulder shatters, a rising/falling pitch-swept oscillator bank during 2-second Chrono-Rewind, and sub-bass seismic thuds when Giant Mega-Rex’s feet hit the canyon floor.

3. Turning Nostalgia Upside Down: Three Meta-Gameplay Twists

Once the core 3D runner felt tight, I asked Gemini Carbon what twists could make players grin while deepening the risk-reward loop. Together we landed on three mechanics that tie the browser lore directly into 3D platforming:

Twist #1: The Reconnecting Wi-Fi Router Satellite Meta-Boss

Think about the existential paradox of Chrome’s Offline Dino: the moment your Wi-Fi reconnects, the page reloads and Rex’s entire universe vanishes. Starting at 620 points (and returning every 1,050 points), a giant 3D voxel Wi-Fi Router Satellite with glowing concentric signal arcs descends ahead of the track while a warning banner flashes WI-FI SIGNAL DETECTED — RECONNECTING....

In WifiBossSystem.ts, the satellite telegraphs and fires sweeping cyan scan-beams down individual lanes while dropping towering ERR_404 monoliths and glowing Signal Relay pylons. Instead of passively surviving, you go on offense: lining up with a Signal Relay and hitting SHIFT (Roar-Dash) shatters the pylon, sparks the satellite, knocks back its reconnection progress, and awards +150 CONNECTION REFUSED!. Jam three relays, and the router spirals back into orbit:

Twist #2: Prehistoric Amber 2.0s Time-Rewind & Your Crowned Ghost Rival

Nothing stings worse in an endless runner than losing a 1,900-point run to a single mistimed lane switch—yet a generic “extra hit-point shield” feels passive. Instead, we placed glowing golden Prehistoric Amber Crystals along risky Tier-1 and Tier-2 sky bridges. Collecting three fragments in ChronoGhostSystem.ts ignites a floating golden Chrono-Orb orbiting Rex’s shoulders.

Meanwhile, ChronoGhostSystem continuously records a 60 Hz ring buffer of your last 130 kinematic frames alongside your best historical run from localStorage—rendering your personal record right on the track as a translucent cyan Holographic Ghost Dino wearing a floating golden voxel crown (which award a celebratory NEW RECORD — GHOST OVERTAKEN! burst the moment you pass it). When you clip a lethal cactus or meteor with an Amber charge armed, time doesn’t just pause—the entire 3D world physically scrubs backward for 2.0 full seconds, pulling obstacles back down the canyon and sucking meteors in reverse up toward the clouds so you can steer cleanly into an open lane: 

// Deterministic 2.0-second reverse timeline scrub in ChronoGhostSystem.ts
const targetIdx = Math.max(0, Math.floor((1 - t) * this.rewindStartSnapshotIndex));
const snap = this.rewindBuffer[targetIdx];
const worldShiftZ = -snap.runSpeed * dt * (CONFIG.REWIND_HISTORY_SEC / CONFIG.REWIND_DURATION_REAL_SEC);

for (const obs of obstacles) obs.z += worldShiftZ; // Push canyon hazards backward down -Z!
for (const m of meteors) {
  m.z += worldShiftZ;
  if (!m.impacted) m.elapsed = Math.max(0, m.elapsed - dt * 2.0); // Pull meteors back into the sky!
}

4. What One-Shotting This Game Revealed About Gemini Carbon

Using this project as a live capability test of Gemini Carbon highlighted four areas where frontier agentic coding has genuinely crossed a threshold:

  1. Narrative-to-Systems Coherence: Rather than bolting random power-ups onto a generic Three.js cube template, Gemini Carbon mapped every story beat directly into math—connecting Chrome’s flat #f7f7f7 offline page to a 75° perspective camera awakening, turning the joke of “reconnecting Wi-Fi” into a 3D laser-sweeping boss fight, and making Act IV’s meteors reshape the platforming topology via obsidian ramps.
  2. Spatial & Visual Taste Without Asset Crutches: Generating believable 3D silhouettes procedurally in code is notoriously unforgiving—one wrong offset turns a T-Rex into a floating pile of boxes. Constructing beveled vertex-colored voxel rigs (VoxelBuilder.ts) with counter-rotating hip joints, ground-projected shadow blobs for jump readability, and wide O_O shocked eyes on collision gave Rex immediate personality in zero kilobytes of external `.glb` assets.
  3. Self-Verifying Physics & Visual QA: By isolating DinoController, CollisionSystem, and StoryDirector from WebGL, Gemini Carbon wrote and passed 19 deterministic Vitest specs (catching subtle 1-tick Euler gravity integration offsets on coyote-time jumps), then drove headless Chrome WebGL screenshots to catch and fix two real visual polish issues on its own: shifting the Game Over card off-center so Rex’s shocked O_O eyes stay visible, and dynamically widening the camera FOV when 2.35× Mega-Rex activates so his giant crest never clips the top HUD.
  4. Instant Zero-Friction Web Distribution: Because every mesh, shader gradient, particle burst, and sound effect is procedural TypeScript, npm run build compiles the entire game into a single static bundle (~152 KB gzipped) that boots in milliseconds from GitHub Pages on both 16:9 desktops and 9:16 mobile portrait screens.
